Fragrance ingredients do not necessarily stay unchanged after release into a room. Some can react with other substances in the air. Understanding that chemistry helps explain why a pleasant smell is not a reliable measure of indoor air quality. It does **not** mean that every scented product creates the same exposure or health risk.

## A specific example: limonene and ozone

Limonene is a citrus-smelling compound used in some fragranced products. In a [dynamic chamber experiment published in *Environmental Health Perspectives*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11133393/), researchers introduced limonene from a chemical source or a scented air freshener, then added ozone. They measured formation of small airborne particles after the reaction. The experiment demonstrates a *possible formation pathway* under the tested conditions; it does not measure disease in people living in ordinary homes.

A later [walk-in chamber study of a cleaning product and a plug-in air freshener](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24769411/) measured emissions and reaction products at two ozone levels. The authors found that results depended on product and ozone conditions, and identified some reaction products of concern under the higher-ozone test condition. Again, the chamber conditions and selected products should not be treated as a universal household exposure estimate.

The [US EPA's indoor particulate matter guidance](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/sources-indoor-particulate-matter-pm) explains that some pine- or citrus-scented products can react with ozone to form particles and formaldehyde indoors. It also recommends following product instructions and considering alternatives when a product causes symptoms.

## What this means for health claims

Measured emissions or particles establish that **exposure can occur**. They do not automatically establish the dose a particular person received or the cause of their symptoms. The [EPA's fragrance-and-health Q&A](https://www.epa.gov/asthma/can-use-fragrances-indoors-cause-health-impacts) acknowledges that sensitive individuals can experience adverse effects while also describing uncertainty about mechanisms behind some reported reactions.

This is why we identify the ingredient, reaction conditions, measured outcome, and study design before drawing a conclusion. The [evidence library](../../evidence/) links the original papers alongside government guidance.

## Practical response

If an air freshener is being used to cover an odor, find and address the underlying source. Reducing unnecessary emissions and using adequate ventilation are reasonable steps supported by [EPA guidance on indoor odors](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2015-09/documents/view_indoors_odors.pdf). If you are managing asthma or a recurrent reaction, work with a clinician on your individual triggers.
