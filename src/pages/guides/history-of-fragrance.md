Fragrance did not suddenly change from harmless flowers to harmful chemicals. Its history is a gradual expansion of ingredients, extraction techniques and industrial manufacturing. Natural materials remain in use, while many modern formulas combine them with synthesized molecules. The useful distinction is between **where an ingredient comes from, how it is made, and what evidence says about exposure**.

## The Middle Ages: plants, resins and scented waters

Medieval scent served religious, medicinal, culinary and cosmetic purposes. Herbs, flowers and resins were prepared through processes such as maceration and filtration. Knowledge of distillation circulated through Arabic scholarship and European medical traditions; the still helped develop scented waters. These practices were not one uniform worldwide tradition. [The International Perfume Museum in Grasse outlines this history](https://www.museesdegrasse.com/en/history-perfumery).

“Natural” did not mean purified, standardized or proven harmless. Historical use is evidence of a practice—not a clinical safety assessment.

## The 1500s–1700s: alcohol, trade and specialist crafts

Alcohol became increasingly important in European perfume-making from the sixteenth century. Trade broadened the palette of aromatic materials. In Grasse, perfumery grew alongside glove-making and eventually became a distinct craft. Changes in ideas about hygiene also changed how scent was used. [Museum history of perfumery](https://www.museesdegrasse.com/en/history-perfumery).

An extraction process separates scented components from raw material. It is different from synthesis, which makes a molecule through chemical reactions. Both involve chemistry; neither word is a complete safety rating.

## The 1800s: chemists begin making aroma molecules

Industrial perfumery expanded the perfumer’s palette beyond what could be harvested and extracted. William Henry Perkin published his artificial production of **coumarin in 1868**; this is a documented early milestone in aroma synthesis. [Read Perkin’s original chemical paper](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlelanding/1868/js/js8682100053).

In **1874**, Tiemann and Haarmann synthesized vanillin from coniferin, a plant-derived material. This example matters: “synthetic” was not automatically synonymous with “petroleum-derived.” [American Chemical Society: vanillin](https://www.acs.org/molecule-of-the-week/archive/v/vanillin.html).

Late-nineteenth-century perfumes began combining synthetic odorants with natural materials. It was an expansion of methods, not the disappearance of botanicals. [International Perfume Museum](https://www.museesdegrasse.com/en/history-perfumery).

## The 1900s: scalable production and everyday scent

Synthesis offered ingredients that could be less expensive and more stable than some natural extracts, as well as new scent possibilities. That helped industrial production serve a much larger market. [Chemistry Perfumes Your Daily Life, Journal of Chemical Education](https://doi.org/10.1021/ed081p45).

The Grasse museum describes increasingly widespread synthetic scents and price competition with natural materials from the 1960s. This economic history explains their adoption; it does not establish that every replacement was toxic. [Museum account of fragrant plants and industry](https://www.museesdegrasse.com/en/conservatory-fragrant-plants).

## Today: why petrochemicals are part of the story

Petrochemical feedstocks are industrial starting materials obtained from petroleum or natural gas. A feedstock is transformed through processing; it is not the same thing as the finished aroma molecule. The ACS describes [petrochemical production within organic chemistry](https://www.acs.org/careers/chemical-sciences/areas/organic-chemistry.html).

Vanillin provides a clear example: the ACS reports widespread production from petrochemical precursors, while also describing alternative production from lignin, a wood-processing byproduct. Extraction, synthesis and newer renewable approaches can coexist. This example is **not** a percentage estimate for all perfume ingredients. [ACS vanillin overview and updates](https://www.acs.org/molecule-of-the-week/archive/v/vanillin.html).

## Why modern fragrance raises health questions

**Manufacturing origin is not a toxicity test.** Chemical risk assessment considers the specific substance, its effects and exposure. Biological responses can occur to natural and synthetic substances alike. [ACS chemical-risk assessment policy](https://www.acs.org/policy/publicpolicies/sustainability/chemicalsmanagement.html).

Fragrance mixtures can contain allergens, including constituents of essential oils. They may also be hard to investigate when individual ingredients are not disclosed. In US cosmetics, scent mixtures can generally be labelled simply “fragrance.” [FDA: fragrances in cosmetics](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-ingredients/fragrances-cosmetics).

Some chemicals have endocrine-disruption evidence, but that does not identify the contents or risk of every fragrance. Phthalates differ: the FDA states that it has no safety concerns about **DEP as currently used in cosmetics and fragrances**. [FDA: phthalates in cosmetics](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-ingredients/phthalates-cosmetics). See our [VOCs and endocrine disruptors guide](/guides/vocs-and-endocrine-disruptors/) for context.

For a sensitive person, reducing optional scent can be a practical choice without proving that every synthetic ingredient is harmful. Use the [fragrance-free product directory](/directory/?scent=no-essential-oils), check the exact local formula, and remember that a natural essential oil is still fragrance—not a scent-free alternative.
