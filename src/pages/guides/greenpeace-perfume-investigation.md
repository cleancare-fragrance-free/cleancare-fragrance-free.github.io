In 2005, Greenpeace published *Perfume: An Investigation of Chemicals in 36 Eaux de Toilette and Eaux de Parfum*. The investigation looked for phthalates and synthetic musks in a random selection of 36 perfume brands. It offers a snapshot of products sampled in 2003–04—not a survey of today’s perfumes.

## What the investigation tested

Greenpeace commissioned the independent Netherlands laboratory TNO Environment and Geosciences to analyse 36 eau de toilette and eau de parfum samples. The report found measurable phthalates in 35 of the 36 samples; diethyl phthalate (DEP) was detected in 34. Polycyclic musks, including galaxolide (HHCB) and tonalide (AHTN), appeared in almost all samples tested for them. Levels varied widely between products.

The report named familiar examples among the samples with high measurements: DEP was highest in Calvin Klein’s Eternity for Women in the tested set; HHCB measurements were high in The Body Shop’s White Musk; and total synthetic-musks readings were high in Jean-Paul Gaultier’s Le Mâle and Cartier’s Le Baiser du Dragon. These are results for the specific samples collected more than two decades ago, not current assessments of those brands or formulas.

## What Greenpeace was concerned about

The report discussed exposure and environmental persistence, and reviewed concerns about possible biological effects of some phthalates and synthetic musks. It described DEP as able to penetrate skin, while also noting that it is rapidly metabolised and that long-term effects from repeated exposure were not well understood. For certain musk compounds, the report discussed emerging evidence of possible endocrine activity. Those statements reflect the evidence and debate available to the authors at the time; they do not establish that ordinary perfume use causes a particular illness.

The investigation’s policy aim was to encourage stronger chemical controls and substitution with safer alternatives. It pointed to the then-developing EU REACH legislation as an opportunity. The report is advocacy research, and its publication alone does not show that it caused any particular regulation to be adopted.

## How to read it today

- Treat this as a historical chemical screen of 36 selected samples, not a representative estimate of today’s market.
- Chemical detection establishes presence in the tested sample; it does not by itself establish a harmful dose or a health outcome in a person.
- Product ingredients, regulations and formulations can change. Do not infer that a perfume sold today has the same composition as a sample tested in 2003–04.
- Evidence and regulatory views have developed since publication. The [US FDA’s current overview of phthalates in cosmetics](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-ingredients/phthalates-cosmetics) says DEP is the phthalate it still commonly sees used in cosmetics, while stating that based on currently available safety information it has no safety concerns about DEP as currently used in cosmetics and fragrances. The FDA also notes that fragrance ingredients may not be individually listed.
