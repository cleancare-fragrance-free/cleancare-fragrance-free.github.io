“VOC,” “endocrine disruptor,” “synthetic,” “natural,” and “organic” are often grouped together in product marketing. They do not mean the same thing. Understanding the distinction helps you ask better questions without assuming that every fragranced product has the same formula, exposure, or risk.

## What is a VOC?

A **volatile organic compound (VOC)** is an organic chemical that can evaporate into the air. The term describes a physical and chemical property, not a complete safety rating. According to the [US EPA’s indoor-air guidance](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/volatile-organic-compounds-impact-indoor-air-quality), paints, cleaners, disinfectants, air fresheners, cosmetics and many other household products can emit organic compounds during use or storage.

Different VOCs have very different toxicities. Concentration, duration, route of exposure and a person’s susceptibility all matter. The EPA cautions that lowering a single “total VOC” number does not automatically make a product safer, because the individual chemicals in that total can differ greatly.

Some pine- or citrus-scented products can also react with ozone indoors and form particles or formaldehyde. That is a specific chemistry pathway described in [EPA guidance on indoor particulate matter](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/sources-indoor-particulate-matter-pm); it is not proof that every scented product creates the same exposure in every room.

## What is an endocrine disruptor?

The endocrine system uses hormones to coordinate processes such as growth, metabolism, reproduction and development. The [US National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences](https://www.niehs.nih.gov/health/topics/agents/endocrine) describes endocrine-disrupting chemicals as substances that may interfere with normal hormone action—for example by mimicking a hormone, blocking a receptor, or changing hormone production or breakdown.

That is a **biological-effect category**, not an air-emissions category. An endocrine-active chemical does not have to be volatile, and a VOC does not have to disrupt hormones. Hazard evidence also does not, by itself, establish the risk from a particular consumer product: exposure level and real-world use still matter.

## Where does fragrance fit?

Fragrance formulas can contain many natural and synthetic ingredients. Under US cosmetic rules, those ingredients may usually appear on the label simply as “fragrance.” The [FDA’s fragrance overview](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-ingredients/fragrances-cosmetics) explains both this trade-secret provision and the potential for fragrance ingredients to cause allergy or sensitivity in some people.

Phthalates are frequently mentioned in endocrine-disruption discussions. Some phthalates have endocrine and reproductive evidence, but **not all phthalates are the same and not every fragrance contains them**. Diethyl phthalate (DEP) has historically been used as a fragrance solvent or fixative. The [FDA’s current phthalates page](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-ingredients/phthalates-cosmetics) says it does not have safety concerns about DEP as currently used in cosmetics and fragrances, while acknowledging that individual fragrance ingredients may not be disclosed on the label. That nuance should not be replaced with “all fragrance contains endocrine disruptors.”

## Are essential oils a safer answer?

“Natural” describes origin, not automatic safety. Essential oils are volatile fragrance mixtures and can contain known contact allergens. NIEHS also notes research into potential endocrine activity of chemicals in lavender and tea tree oils. This does not establish that every use causes a hormonal effect, but it does mean “natural essential oil” should not be used as a synonym for fragrance-free or risk-free.

For strict fragrance avoidance, choose products that make an explicit fragrance-free claim and do not add essential oils for scent.

## Does “organic” settle the question?

Organic certification can provide useful rules for agricultural production, sourcing and processing, but the exact standard and product category matter. A certified ingredient or product can still have an aroma, emit VOCs, or contain an essential oil. “Organic,” “natural,” and “clean” therefore do not answer the same question as “fragrance-free.”

Look for the certification body, read its actual standard, and check the complete product label. Do not infer a medical safety guarantee from a front-of-pack claim.

## Practical choices that follow the evidence

1. **Reduce optional scent sources first.** Air fresheners, scent boosters, candles and diffusers intentionally add emissions to indoor air.
2. **Choose the exact fragrance-free product.** Do not generalise from a brand name or from “unscented.”
3. **Ventilate during necessary product use.** Follow label directions and never mix household cleaners unless the label explicitly instructs it.
4. **Use environmental labels precisely.** The [EPA Safer Choice label](https://www.epa.gov/saferchoice/learn-about-safer-choice-label), including its fragrance-free designation, evaluates ingredients against defined criteria; it is more informative than an undefined “green” claim.
5. **Keep health claims proportional.** A chemical hazard, measured emission, biomarker or laboratory effect is not automatically proof that a consumer product caused a person’s symptoms or disease.

The useful question is not “chemical or natural?” Everything is made of chemicals. Ask instead: **Which ingredient? What exposure? What evidence? What uncertainty? What practical alternative?**
