Fragrance is not one chemical, and perfume is not the only relevant product. Scent ingredients can appear in personal care, cleaning and household products. Environmental impact depends on the specific ingredients, how much is used, how they are released, local air and water systems, packaging, and the full product lifecycle. The most useful question is not “is every fragrance environmentally toxic?” but **which pathways and ingredients are being discussed?**

## Air: volatile products can contribute to urban pollution

Many consumer products are designed to release volatile organic compounds (VOCs). In the atmosphere, some VOCs react with nitrogen oxides and other compounds, contributing to ground-level ozone and particulate matter. That is an air-quality issue, not proof that every scented product creates the same outdoor exposure.

A 2018 study highlighted by [NOAA](https://csl.noaa.gov/news/2018/226_0215.html) found that volatile chemical products—including personal-care products, cleaning agents, coatings and other products—accounted for about half of fossil-fuel VOC emissions in the industrialised cities analysed. It is often shortened to “perfume equals cars,” which is too broad: the study evaluated a large category of products and emissions, not the effect of one perfume or one household.

Reducing optional scent products can be one way to reduce a household’s contribution, alongside using products sparingly and following local air-quality guidance.

## Water: some ingredients travel down the drain

When rinse-off products are used, residues enter wastewater. Research has detected certain synthetic musks in wastewater, surface water, sediment and aquatic organisms. A [2024 review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38199360/) describes widespread environmental detection and notes that incomplete degradation can contribute to accumulation in aquatic systems.

This does **not** mean every fragrance ingredient persists or bioaccumulates, nor that a detection alone proves harm at a particular location. It does show why substance-by-substance assessment and wastewater monitoring matter. A separate [review of polycyclic musks](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30326456/) notes that sewage treatment does not completely remove some of these compounds.

Avoid pouring unused perfumes, oils or cleaners down a drain. Follow local household-hazardous-waste and product-disposal instructions instead.

## Resources and manufacturing: origin is not a full environmental score

Modern fragrance formulas may contain ingredients made from petrochemical feedstocks, plant materials, or both. Each route has trade-offs. Petrochemical production has fossil-resource implications; plant-derived ingredients can have land, water, farming and biodiversity impacts. Packaging, transport and how much product is used can also outweigh a simple “natural versus synthetic” label.

The [European Commission’s Joint Research Centre](https://susproc.jrc.ec.europa.eu/product-bureau/sites/default/files/2023-01/Preliminary%20ESPR%20WP%20Report_MERGED_CLEAN_.pdf) notes that cosmetics can have environmental impacts across the lifecycle and discusses lower- or no-VOC formulations, dosage and fragrance-free options. It is a policy-scoping document, not a ranking of individual products.

## What a lower-impact approach can look like

- Remove optional air fresheners and scent boosters before replacing essential products.
- Choose the exact fragrance-free formula when it works for the task; check the current local label.
- Use only the amount needed, favour concentrated or refill formats where they genuinely reduce packaging and transport, and avoid wasteful duplicate products.
- Keep unused liquids out of drains and follow local disposal rules.
- Ask brands for formula transparency and credible, product-specific environmental information.

Fragrance-free does not guarantee a low-impact product. It is one practical choice that can remove an optional scent source while leaving room to assess the rest of the formula and lifecycle.
