An air purifier can be useful in some indoor-air situations, but it cannot make an ongoing source disappear. Start by identifying the source of the odour or VOCs—such as an air freshener, fragranced product, paint, solvent, cleaning product, smoke, moisture problem or newly introduced material—then reduce or remove it where possible.

The [US EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/air-cleaners-and-air-filters-home) puts the order clearly: source control and ventilation with clean outdoor air are the most effective ways to improve indoor air; filtration can be a supplement. No portable cleaner removes every pollutant from a room.

## HEPA and VOCs solve different problems

**HEPA filters are designed for particles**—for example dust, pollen and some smoke particles. VOCs are gases, so a standard HEPA filter alone is not designed to capture them.

For gas-phase pollutants, air cleaners may use a **sorbent** medium such as activated carbon. Its porous surface can adsorb some gas molecules. Performance depends on the specific VOC, the amount and type of sorbent material, airflow, room conditions, filter age and the ongoing source. A thin carbon-coated sheet is not automatically comparable with a unit that contains a meaningful bed of sorbent media.

## What the MIT study found

An [MIT-led chamber study](https://news.mit.edu/2021/study-finds-indoor-air-cleaners-fall-short-removing-volatile-organic-compounds-1029) tested four consumer-grade **oxidation-based** air cleaners. VOC removal varied widely. In the better-performing units, physical sorbents or filters did most of the removal; the oxidation step contributed little or could create byproducts. The study did not test ordinary HEPA particle performance.

That result supports a practical buying question: if VOC reduction is the goal, ask what gas-phase media the device contains, whether the manufacturer publishes VOC test conditions and results, and what replacement schedule it specifies. Avoid assuming a “HEPA” badge or a broad “chemical removal” claim answers those questions.

## A careful selection checklist

- First, remove or reduce the scent source and ventilate appropriately. Do not buy a purifier to keep using an avoidable fragrance source.
- For particles, look for a correctly sized HEPA-capable cleaner and a published clean-air delivery rate (CADR) for the room.
- For gases, look for clearly described activated-carbon or other sorbent media and product-specific VOC performance information—not just generic marketing language.
- Be cautious with devices marketed around oxidation, ionisation or ozone. The MIT study found some oxidation-based units produced VOCs or byproducts during operation.
- Plan for filter replacement. Adsorbent media becomes saturated; a purifier is not a permanent “set and forget” solution.

## When a purifier is not enough

Strong, persistent or unexplained odours can signal a building, moisture, combustion or product problem that needs investigation. If symptoms are severe or persistent, do not rely on a purifier as medical treatment—seek advice from a qualified professional.

For a fragrance-aware home, begin with the lowest-cost prevention: skip air fresheners and scent boosters, choose the exact fragrance-free product version, ventilate during activities that generate pollutants, and resolve the underlying source of unwanted odours.
