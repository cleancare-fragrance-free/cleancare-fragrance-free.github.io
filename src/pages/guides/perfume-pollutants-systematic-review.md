This guide adds a peer-reviewed, open-access paper to CleanCare’s reading list: [Kazemi et al., “Evaluation of pollutants in perfumes, colognes and health effects on the consumer”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9163252/), published in 2022. It is a systematic review of literature published through August 2020. The authors screened 562 records and included 37 studies.

The paper is useful because it brings together discussions of ingredients, reported symptoms and potential health concerns. It should not be treated as a diagnosis tool or as proof that wearing perfume causes the illnesses named in its summary figure.

## What the review examined

The authors reviewed literature about compounds reported in perfumes and colognes—including some phthalates, aldehydes, parabens and fragrance materials—and described reported adverse effects. Their article discusses contact allergy, respiratory symptoms, headaches, endocrine-related questions, reproductive outcomes and cancer-related claims.

That is a wide scope. It means the review gathers different kinds of evidence: chemical measurements, questionnaire studies, clinical allergy observations, laboratory findings and observational associations. Those study types answer different questions and cannot all establish the same level of causation.

## What the paper’s disease graphic can—and cannot—show

The figure attached to this reading is the paper’s visual summary of conditions discussed in the literature. It includes cancer, fertility, thyroid, skin and respiratory labels. It is **not** an individual risk chart, and the picture of a condition beside a perfume bottle does not establish that the bottle causes that condition in every user.

For example, an ingredient can be detected in a product or human tissue without proving that it caused a disease. An association in an observational study may be influenced by other exposures. Results from an ingredient, a high-dose laboratory setting or an occupational context may not apply directly to normal consumer use. The paper itself draws from heterogeneous sources, so its claims need to be traced back to their individual studies before using them in health communication.

For that reason, CleanCare does not reproduce the figure as a general health-warning graphic. Readers can inspect it in the [open-access original article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9163252/#Fig2), with the authors’ captions and cited references.

## What is more firmly established

Fragrance ingredients are recognised causes of **allergic contact dermatitis** in some people. That is different from saying every fragranced product is harmful or that each reported symptom has a single cause. The [FDA’s overview of fragrances in cosmetics](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-ingredients/fragrances-cosmetics) explains that some people may be sensitive or allergic and notes that ingredients in cosmetic fragrances can be listed as “fragrance.”

Other health questions require ingredient-specific, exposure-specific evidence. The FDA, for example, states that it has no safety concerns about **diethyl phthalate (DEP)** as currently used in cosmetics and fragrances, even though other phthalates have different evidence and regulatory histories. [Read the FDA’s phthalate guidance](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-ingredients/phthalates-cosmetics).

## Why reviews can disagree

Evidence reviews can weigh studies differently. A separate 2019 review on fragrance inhalation concluded that many claimed inhalation effects had not been confirmed by sufficiently rigorous epidemiology; it also identifies its authors’ consultancy affiliations. [Read that review and its disclosures](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30904429/). Its conclusion does not erase personal reactions or established skin allergy; it shows why strong claims need careful, transparent evidence.

When comparing reviews, check the search dates, what evidence they included, whether they assess study quality, their funding and conflicts of interest, and whether a conclusion concerns an ingredient, a product, a route of exposure or a specific population.

## A practical way to use this source

- Use it to identify questions and follow its citations—not as a stand-alone disease list.
- If you experience a rash, persistent respiratory symptoms or another health concern, discuss it with a qualified clinician rather than self-diagnosing from an infographic.
- Choose a fragrance-free product when avoiding added scent is useful for you, and remember that fragrance-free is not a promise of zero risk or suitability for every person.
- Share the original paper with context: it is an open-access review with broad claims that require study-by-study checking.
