Changing your own products is one thing. Talking about someone else’s perfume or an air freshener in a shared room can feel more difficult. Start with a specific request, made early and without guessing another person’s intentions.

## Ask for one practical change

You might ask a host not to use a scented candle during your visit, or ask whether a meeting room has a plug-in air freshener. Describe what would help you participate.

For example: “Would you be comfortable leaving the diffuser off while I’m here? I’ve noticed symptoms around scented products, and I’m trying to reduce those exposures.”

This is original suggested wording, not a quotation from a community member or a claim that everyone responds in the same way.

## Make expectations clear

The [Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety’s workplace guidance](https://www.ccohs.ca/oshanswers/hsprograms/scent_free.html) recommends consultation, education, and clear communication when developing a scent-free policy. It considers cleaners and air fresheners as well as personal perfume.

At work, ask the relevant manager or occupational-health contact about the existing process. A personal request and a formal policy are different things. This article is not legal advice about accommodation requirements.

## Think beyond the perfume bottle

A room may have several scent sources. Ask what is used before choosing a solution.

The [EPA’s indoor-air guidance](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/improving-indoor-air-quality) prioritises reducing pollutant sources and considers ventilation and filtration as additional approaches. Outdoor pollution or smoke can limit the benefit of opening windows. An air purifier does not guarantee removal of all gases or scents.

## Keep the conversation open

Agree on a manageable change and check in afterwards. Respect privacy: a considerate conversation does not need to involve someone’s full medical history. People may need different arrangements, and symptoms can have several causes.
