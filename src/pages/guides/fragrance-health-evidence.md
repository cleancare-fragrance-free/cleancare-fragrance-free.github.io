“Fragrance” can mean a mixture of ingredients rather than one substance. It may appear in perfumes, cosmetics, laundry products, cleaners, and room fresheners. The ingredients, amount used, route of exposure, and individual susceptibility all matter. A claim that **every synthetic fragrance is toxic at everyday exposure** would go beyond the evidence reviewed here. A more useful question is: *which effects are documented, for whom, and under what conditions?*

## Where exposure begins

In the United States, cosmetic ingredient lists may identify a mixture simply as “fragrance” or “flavor,” rather than naming every component. The [US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) explains the labeling rule and its limits](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-ingredients/fragrances-cosmetics). Fragrance is also used in products outside cosmetics, including laundry and room fresheners, which may follow different labeling rules.

For indoor air, the [US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) lists air fresheners among possible sources of volatile organic compounds (VOCs)](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/volatile-organic-compounds-impact-indoor-air-quality). “VOC” describes how a substance enters air; it is not a single health-risk category. The same EPA page says the health effects of organic chemicals vary greatly and depend on factors such as exposure level and duration.

<h2 id="health-effects">What health effects are supported?</h2>

**Contact allergy is well established for some fragrance ingredients.** The [FDA lists fragrances among common cosmetic allergens](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-ingredients/allergens-cosmetics) and describes allergic contact dermatitis as a possible reaction. A [population study across five European countries](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26332456/) used patch tests and estimated a conservative fragrance contact allergy prevalence of 1.9% in its study population. That figure is not a global rate and should not be applied to every fragrance ingredient.

**Some people report respiratory symptoms around fragrances.** The FDA notes that people sensitive to certain fragrances may experience symptoms such as coughing, chest tightness, or wheezing when inhaling them. A reported symptom does not, by itself, identify the precise chemical or prove the same effect in everyone.

**Indoor air exposures deserve context.** The EPA describes irritation and other possible effects from *some* VOCs, while also noting that the health effects of the concentrations usually found in homes are not well characterized. It would be inaccurate to transfer a hazard identified for one solvent, at one dose, to all scented consumer products.

## What does “synthetic” tell us?

The source of an ingredient—made from plants or synthesized—does not establish its safety. The FDA says ingredients from plants are treated the same as ingredients from other sources in cosmetics regulation. An essential oil can contain fragrance ingredients, and a synthetic ingredient can have a very different profile from another synthetic ingredient. The relevant questions are **identity, exposure, and individual response**, not whether a label sounds natural.

## How to read a health claim

When you encounter a claim in a video, article, or social post, look for the substance named, how exposure was measured, the people or model studied, and the outcome measured. A laboratory result, an occupational exposure, and an everyday household exposure answer different questions.
